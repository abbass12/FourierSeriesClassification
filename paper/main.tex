\Title{Fourier Features and Concentration-Factor Jump Descriptors for Synthetic Signal Classification: A Reproducible Pilot Study}

\Author{Abbass Srour}
\AuthorNames{Abbass Srour}
\address{University of Michigan}
\Correspondence{abbasss@umich.edu}

\abstract{This reproducible pilot study examines three representations for classifying synthetic one-dimensional signals: raw samples, truncated Fourier coefficients, and Fourier coefficients augmented with jump descriptors inferred by concentration factors. The implementation is based on a stratified train-validation-test split, seed-controlled data generation, validation-based early stopping, and preserved source code. In a three-seed synthetic smoke experiment comprising five balanced signal families, 80 examples per family, and 12 training epochs, raw samples and Fourier features produced the same mean test accuracy (87.9\%), whereas the Fourier-plus-jump model achieved 80.4\%. In a separate three-seed fixed-split screening on the public ECG200 benchmark, the Fourier model achieved 91.7\% mean test accuracy, compared with 89.3\% for raw samples and 79.0\% for the Fourier-plus-jump model. These preliminary results do not support a claim that the particular inferred jump-feature implementation improves classification accuracy. Rather, they show that conclusions based on a single split and a single random initialization are unstable and that the usefulness of concentration-factor features is an empirical question. The manuscript provides the mathematical construction, a reproducible experimental protocol, software tests, and a preregistered confirmatory evaluation plan using repeated random seeds and external time-series benchmarks. It is a pre-submission draft and must not be submitted as a final research article until the planned confirmatory experiments have been completed.}

\section{Introduction}

Fourier representations provide a mathematically natural way to describe signals by their frequency content~\cite{stein2003fourier,oppenheim2010dsp}. In parallel, modern time-series classification has developed strong empirical baselines, including convolutional architectures, ensembles, and random convolutional-kernel methods~\cite{bagnall2017bakeoff,fawaz2019review,fawaz2020inceptiontime,dempster2020rocket}. These two perspectives motivate a focused question: for a controlled signal-classification task, how does a compact Fourier representation compare with raw samples, and does explicit information about discontinuities add predictive value?

A discontinuity is not localized in a truncated Fourier representation in the same manner as it is in the original signal. Nevertheless, concentration-factor methods can recover edge information from spectral data under appropriate piecewise-smoothness assumptions~\cite{gelb1999edges,gelb2000nonlinear}. This work evaluates a simple machine-learning use of those inferred descriptors: peak locations and signed magnitudes of the generalized conjugate partial Fourier sum are appended to Fourier-coefficient features.

The study is deliberately limited to parameterized synthetic signals. It is therefore not evidence of clinical, industrial, seismic, or other real-world performance. Its contribution is an auditable experimental framework that separates a mathematical feature-extraction hypothesis from a performance claim. All generation, feature extraction, training, evaluation, figures, test results, and planned confirmatory procedures are distributed with the manuscript~\cite{srour2026code}.

\section{Mathematical Framework}

\subsection{Fourier Representation}

Let $f \in L^2(-\pi,\pi)$ be extended $2\pi$-periodically. Its Fourier coefficients and the truncated partial sum are
\begin{equation}
\widehat{f}_k = \frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{-\pi}^{\pi} f(x)e^{-ikx}\,dx, \qquad
S_N f(x) = \sum_{|k|\leq N}\widehat{f}_k e^{ikx}.
\end{equation}

For a sampled signal, we estimate coefficients with the discrete Fourier transform implemented by the Fast Fourier Transform~\cite{oppenheim2010dsp}. The real and imaginary parts of the selected $N$ coefficients are concatenated, resulting in a $2N$-dimensional real-valued feature vector.

\subsection{Jump Descriptors from Concentration Factors}

For a piecewise-smooth $f$, define the jump function at $x$ by
\begin{equation}
[f](x) = f(x^+) - f(x^-).
\end{equation}

Following Gelb and Tadmor~\cite{gelb1999edges,gelb2000nonlinear}, a concentration-factor edge approximation takes the form
\begin{equation}
E_N^{\sigma}f(x) = i\sum_{|k|\leq N}\widehat{f}_k\,\sgn(k)\,\sigma\!\left(\frac{|k|}{N}\right)e^{ikx},
\label{eq:concentration}
\end{equation}
where $\sigma$ is a concentration factor. Under the assumptions given in the cited theory, the operator concentrates near jumps. The distributed implementation includes trigonometric, polynomial, and exponential concentration factors. The pilot experiment uses the trigonometric factor.

The classifier cannot consume a variable number of peaks directly. For every signal, the peak detector sorts candidate jumps by absolute edge magnitude, retains up to four location-magnitude pairs, and pads missing entries with zeros. These descriptors should be interpreted as a practical feature-engineering choice, not as an exact recovery theorem for noisy, sampled observations.

\section{Materials and Methods}

\subsection{Synthetic Dataset}

Signals are sampled on 1500 equally spaced points in $[-\pi,\pi)$. Five balanced families are used: a compactly supported sinusoid, box pulse, sawtooth ramp, compactly supported exponential, and compactly supported Gaussian. Within each family, support width and shape parameters are sampled from fixed class-specific distributions. Each signal is normalized to unit maximum absolute amplitude. Gaussian white noise can be added at a prescribed signal-to-noise ratio; the pilot result below uses the clean condition.

A stratified 70\%/10\%/20\% train-validation-test split is used so that every balanced class contributes equally to every partition. Python, NumPy, and PyTorch pseudorandom generators are seeded per run. The implementation uses NumPy~\cite{harris2020numpy}, SciPy~\cite{virtanen2020scipy}, and PyTorch~\cite{paszke2019pytorch}.

\subsection{Models}

Table~\ref{tab:models} gives the three models. Models A and B share a feed-forward architecture with hidden widths 256, 128, and 64, batch normalization, ReLU activations, and dropout. Model C processes the jump feature vector in a 32-unit branch before concatenating it with Fourier features; the resulting vector is passed through the same 256-128-64 classification stack. All models use cross-entropy loss, Adam optimization, a learning-rate scheduler, and validation-loss early stopping. The checkpoint with the lowest validation loss is restored before testing.

\subsection{Pilot Validation Protocol}

The completed smoke experiment uses three independent seeds ($11$, $23$, and $37$), 80 examples per class, 12 training epochs, batch size 32, and $N=50$ Fourier modes. This small run was designed to test reproducibility plumbing, not to estimate final benchmark performance. For each seed, all models are trained independently and evaluated on the held-out test partition generated with the same seed. We report the mean, sample standard deviation, and a normal-approximation 95\% confidence interval across the three seeds. Two-sided paired Wilcoxon tests are reported descriptively; with only three seeds they are severely underpowered and must not be treated as confirmatory inference.

\subsection{Public-Benchmark Screening Protocol}

As an initial external-data check, we evaluated the same representations on ECG200 from the UCR Time Series Archive~\cite{dau2019ucr}. The public dataset contains a fixed 100-series training set and 100-series test set, with two classes and 96 samples per series. The archive train/test split was preserved. For each of three seeds, we stratified only the training set into 80 training and 20 validation series, z-normalized each series independently, and trained the models for up to 50 epochs with validation early stopping. The Fourier and jump settings were 32 modes, the trigonometric concentration factor, four retained peaks, and the combined location-and-magnitude descriptor. This external screening is small and must not be interpreted as a clinical result or a comparison with UCR leaderboards.

\section{Discussion}

Neither the synthetic pilot nor the ECG200 screening reproduces an apparent advantage for inferred jump features reported by earlier one-off experiments. This mismatch is scientifically important. It may arise from small-sample noise, changes in the split and early-stopping implementation, insufficient training of Model C, a suboptimal peak-extraction design, or the fact that the raw/Fourier representation already contains the relevant discriminative information for these synthetic classes. The current evidence does not distinguish these explanations.

The absence of a demonstrated pilot benefit does not invalidate concentration-factor theory. Equation~\eqref{eq:concentration} concerns the extraction of discontinuity information from spectral data under mathematical assumptions; it does not imply that a particular neural-network architecture will improve classification accuracy after zero padding, peak selection, and finite-sample optimization. This distinction should guide any later performance claim.

The comparison is also narrow relative to modern time-series classification. Strong external baselines include InceptionTime~\cite{fawaz2020inceptiontime} and ROCKET~\cite{dempster2020rocket}, and the broader literature evaluates on standardized collections such as the UCR/UEA archive~\cite{dau2019ucr,bagnall2017bakeoff,fawaz2019review}. A publication-quality study must add these comparisons or clearly restrict its claim to an educational synthetic benchmark. Fourier-based neural approaches, including Fourier neural operators, show that spectral parameterizations remain an active design space, but they address a different function-learning setting and are not a substitute for direct classification baselines~\cite{li2021fno}.

\section{Confirmatory Evaluation Plan}

Before external submission, the following preregistered protocol must be completed and its raw outputs incorporated into the manuscript:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item Run at least ten independent seeds with at least 1000 examples per class on the specified synthetic generator, including clean and prespecified noise conditions.
    \item Select all hyperparameters using validation data only and report the full search space, selection rule, and compute environment.
    \item Compare Models A--C against at least a one-dimensional CNN or InceptionTime-style baseline and a ROCKET/MiniROCKET-style baseline under matched splits.
    \item Replicate the completed one-dataset concentration-factor screening (trigonometric, polynomial, and exponential) and the three-setting ECG200 jump-feature ablation across the full benchmark suite, including mode-count and peak-count sensitivity analyses.
    \item Evaluate at least one public real or standard benchmark dataset, beginning with a documented fixed-split UCR-format dataset~\cite{dau2019ucr}. Claims must be limited to the domains actually evaluated.
    \item Report per-seed results, mean $\pm$ standard deviation, confidence intervals, effect sizes, paired tests, class-wise metrics, confusion matrices, runtime, and parameter counts.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Conclusions}

The supplied codebase provides a reproducible framework for testing Fourier and concentration-factor representations in signal classification. The completed synthetic and ECG200 screening studies establish implementation reproducibility and demonstrate that the prior single-run conclusion was not robust. It does not establish a performance advantage for jump descriptors or a general signal-classification method. The confirmatory plan is therefore a prerequisite for submission, rather than an optional extension.
